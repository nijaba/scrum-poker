# Why scrum poker?

Scrum poker (also called planning poker) is a simple way for a team to estimate work together.
Everyone picks a card in secret, the cards turn over at the same time, and the team talks
about the gap between the highest and lowest card before agreeing on a number. It takes a few
minutes per ticket, and it tends to give better estimates and better conversations than
estimating out loud.

## What it gives your team

### Everyone thinks for themselves first

When people estimate out loud, the first number said shapes every number after it. The most
senior or most confident person speaks, and the rest of the room drifts towards them. Secret
cards remove that pull: each person commits to their own view before they see anyone
else's. In this app no one, not even the facilitator, sees a vote before the reveal; the table
only shows who has voted.

### Disagreement comes out early, when it is cheap

A spread of cards, say a 2 next to a 13, is the most useful thing the reveal can show. It
usually means two people picture different work: one knows about a migration the other
forgot, or one has found a shortcut. Talking it through before the sprint is far cheaper than
finding out halfway through it. The person with the outlier card often knows something the
rest of the team does not.

### The quiet voices count as much as the loud ones

Everyone gets one card, and every card turns over together. A new team member's estimate sits
on the table next to the tech lead's and gets the same attention. People who would not
interrupt a discussion still get their view heard.

### The whole team shares the plan

When the team agrees on a number together, the estimate belongs to all of them, not to the one
person who was asked. People understand what each ticket involves, which helps when someone
else picks it up, and makes the sprint plan something the team believes in.

### Relative sizes are easier than hours

The default cards follow the Fibonacci scale (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13...); rooms can also use
T-shirt sizes or their own list. The gaps widen as the numbers grow, because large pieces of
work are less certain. Asking "is this bigger or smaller than that one?" is something people
answer well; asking "how many hours?" is something they answer badly. A card at the top of
the scale is also a useful signal that the ticket should be split, and the app suggests it. The **?** card lets someone say "I don't know enough to estimate this" without pretending
otherwise, which is often a sign the ticket needs more detail.

### It keeps refinement moving

A fixed routine (read the ticket, vote, reveal, discuss, agree) keeps a backlog session from
wandering. The optional countdown keeps discussion to a sensible length, and a consensus on
the first reveal means the team can move straight on.

## What this app adds

- **Your backlog, in order.** The room loads the Jira tickets that still need an estimate, in
  your backlog's rank order, so nobody has to copy tickets in or decide what comes next.
- **Points go straight back to Jira.** When the team agrees, the facilitator confirms and the
  story points are written to the ticket. No one has to update the tickets after the meeting.
- **Nothing to install, no account for players.** Players open a link and type a name; only the
  facilitators sign in.
- **Works for remote and hybrid teams.** Everyone sees the same table and the same reveal at the
  same moment, wherever they are.

## Tips for a good session

- Discuss the extremes. After a spread, ask the highest and lowest cards to explain first.
- Vote again after the discussion rather than averaging. The goal is a shared understanding,
  not a number in the middle.
- Don't argue over neighbours. If the cards land on 5 and 8, pick one and move on.
- Treat a **?** as a question about the ticket, not about the person.
- Estimate the whole team's effort, not just your own part of the work.
